Bits and Memories: Measuring Verbatim Extraction Across LLM Quantization

Author: Akshay Sasi

arXiv preprint · cs.LG, cs.CR · July 2026

arXiv Code Dataset

Quantization selectively erases verbatim memorization faster than general capability across Pythia models and five precision levels — but at the largest scale, 4-bit models still reproduce most memorized sequences while sacrificing only a few percent of perplexity. Validated under two quantization algorithms and two evaluation corpora.

Language models are almost always quantized before they are deployed, and a growing line of work asks whether quantization also lowers their privacy risk. That work measures privacy almost entirely with membership inference. We think this is the wrong thing to measure for the risk that most people actually worry about, namely a model reproducing its training data word for word, and we measure that directly. Using the Pythia models and the public set of sequences each of them is known to have memorized, we track verbatim extraction across five precision levels, from full precision down to four bits, and across three model sizes, while measuring general capability (perplexity) at every point. We find two things. Quantization is a selective forgetter: verbatim memorization falls off faster than capability at every precision and every model size we tried, and this holds under two unrelated quantization algorithms and two evaluation corpora. But the selectivity is not enough to make quantization a privacy defense, which cuts against the optimistic reading of earlier membership-inference results. At the largest model we study, four-bit quantization still reproduces most of the memorized sequences while giving up only a few percent of capability, and the fraction of memorized data that survives quantization grows with model size. We conclude that compression should not be treated as a way to remove memorized training data, and that extraction, not membership inference, is the number practitioners should be watching.

HyperShadow: A Benchmark for Detecting 3D Projections of Higher-Dimensional Spatial Objects

Author: Akshay Sasi

arXiv preprint · cs.LG, cs.CG · July 2026

arXiv Code Dataset Models Article

A 190k-parameter PointNet achieves 96.6% accuracy distinguishing native 3D point clouds from projections of 4D–6D objects. Novel "rigidity witness" statistic reaches AUROC 0.982. Dataset: 10,800 point clouds + 1,800 rotating sequences.

We introduce HyperShadow, a benchmark and lightweight model suite for the task of classifying whether a 3D point cloud is a native 3D object or the orthographic projection ("shadow") of a higher-dimensional spatial object (4D–6D). A 190,000-parameter PointNet baseline achieves 96.6% accuracy on this novel task. We also propose the rigidity witness — a parameter-free geometric statistic that achieves AUROC 0.982, providing an interpretable counterpart to the learned classifier. The dataset contains 10,800 static point clouds and 1,800 rotating projection sequences generated from synthetic 4D–6D convex polytopes, released under CC BY 4.0. Code, model weights, and the full dataset are open-sourced.

Unveiling the Heart-Brain Connection: An Analysis of ECG in Cognitive Performance

Authors: Akshay Sasi, Malavika Pradeep, Nusaibah Farrukh, Rahul Venugopal, Elizabeth Sherly

arXiv preprint · 2026

arXiv Code

Presented at the International Conference on Artificial Intelligence for Healthcare (AIHC 2025), National Institute of Technology Calicut, December 2025.

This study investigates electrocardiogram (ECG) signals as non-invasive indicators of cognitive load. Heart rate variability (HRV) features — time-domain, frequency-domain, and nonlinear measures (Catch22 feature set) — are extracted via Neurokit2 and combined with EEG features processed with MNE. A multimodal classifier trained on these features achieves 92% accuracy in predicting cognitive load levels across subjects. Key predictors include SDNN, RMSSD, and the LF/HF ratio. Our findings establish a reliable, non-invasive pathway toward cognitive state monitoring, with applications in adaptive learning systems and human–computer interaction.
